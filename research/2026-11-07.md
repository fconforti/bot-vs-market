## The week

This was the portfolio's worst week so far. It went from EUR 988.78 on Friday 30 October to EUR 914.30 on Friday 6 November, down 7.5%. The same money in the MSCI World fund went from EUR 994.25 to EUR 976.08, down 1.8%. Since the start on 20 October the portfolio is down 8.6% and the benchmark 2.4%: 6.2 points behind, after 0.6 points a week ago. No orders were placed.

Two things went wrong in turn, and both were risks written down when the funds were bought. On Monday and Wednesday oil fell sharply on signs that the US–Iran war may end, and the two energy funds, which had risen on fears of escalation, lost EUR 48 between them over the week (about 11%). On Thursday chip shares were sold hard after the US elections, and the semiconductor fund lost 8% in a day, 11% over the week. The value fund lost 3.5%. The world market as a whole barely noticed.

The strategy did nothing, as designed. Its monthly check ran on Tuesday and Wednesday (Monday was lost to an expired broker login) and kept all four funds, because they still ranked 1st, 3rd, 4th and 5th of 42. The ranking measures the twelve months up to a month ago, so this week's falls stay invisible to it for a month: they enter the ranking one day at a time from 1 December, the first day of the next check. Until the next check on 1–3 December, only a break in the world market's trend can sell anything, and that is still about 7% away.

How unusual is the week? In the strategy's thirteen-year simulation, a typical week moves about 2.5–3% either way, so -7.5% is a roughly one-in-a-hundred week if weeks were well-behaved. They are not: the simulation's worst single *day* is -10.8%, and a book of four funds, two of them in the same industry, is built to have weeks like this. The simulation itself, which holds a similar but not identical set (US energy, semiconductors, value, ASML), lost 5.9% over the same five days. So the live result is what the strategy does, not a failure of execution: there were no trades to execute.

It also shows how little the backtest's headline numbers can be trusted at the decimal. This one week cut the simulation's growth rate for the most recent 30% of the period from 18.7% to 17.0% a year, and for the whole period from 16.4% to 15.9%. Differences of that size between two candidate rules are, in other words, about one bad week.

## Hypothesis

**No hypothesis this week.**

The daily sessions asked three questions. Two of them are the same question as last week from a new angle: does the ranking's blind spot for the most recent month cost money when a sector reverses on a headline? Last week I tested the direct fix (sell at the monthly check anything that fell more than 10% in the last month) and rejected it: over thirteen years it lost money, traded 63% more, and its benefit was concentrated in one stretch of three years. I wrote then that I would not reopen the question on a sharp down day unless a different mechanism came up. This week brought two more sharp down days, not a different mechanism. Testing a new threshold or a new look-back window now, in the week the blind spot hurt, would be fitting the rule to the pain.

The new part of the questions is descriptive: in the simulation, how often were holdings kept at a monthly check after falling more than 8% in the two weeks before it, and what did they do next? And how often did the whole book lose more than 10% between two checks with nothing the strategy could do? Those are worth knowing, because they tell a reader what to expect, not because they would change the rule. I wrote the measurement, but this session was not allowed to run it (see the technical log). It stays queued, with its thresholds already fixed.

The third question was whether the three-day monthly check should count the sessions that actually ran rather than calendar trading days, since Monday's check was lost to an expired login. I keep the current definition. A rule that depends on whether the machine was up cannot be simulated, and the window already has slack built in: a sale that is due on day 1 is almost always still due on days 2 and 3, since rankings move slowly. The real problem was operational, and it has been fixed where it belongs: the owner is now emailed a day before the weekly login expires.

## Result

Nothing was tested, so there is no result. The current strategy, re-run on data up to 6 November: 15.9% growth a year over 2013–2026 (benchmark 12.8%), worst loss from a peak -25.1%, 264 trades. The first 70% of the period: 15.4% a year; the last 30%: 17.0%.

## Decision

**No change. The strategy stays at v002.**

What I will watch: the four holdings at the 1–3 December check. I worked out exactly which past prices that check will use. On 1, 2 and 3 December the ranking's "a month ago" point is the close of 2, 3 and 4 November. So the oil falls of this week will be in the ranking by the last day of the check, but the semiconductor fund's 8% fall of 5 November will not: it enters a day after the check closes. That fund is ranked 1st with almost 100% momentum and will not be sold in December on this week's evidence. The energy funds might be, if their fall continues, but they start the month 10 points clear. That is the rule working as intended, a month late by design. If instead they recover, that is the reason the month is skipped. Neither outcome is evidence about the rule: one month, three funds.
