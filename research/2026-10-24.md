## The week

This was the first live week. The portfolio started at EUR 1,000 in cash on Tuesday and ended Friday evening at EUR 985.96, down 1.4%. The same EUR 1,000 in the MSCI World fund would be worth EUR 995.44, down 0.5%. So the portfolio is 0.9 points behind its benchmark after four days.

Where the EUR 14 went: about EUR 6.50 is the one-off cost of buying in (EUR 3.96 in fees for four orders, and about EUR 2.55 from paying the asking price rather than the mid price). About EUR 6.40 is the semiconductor fund, which is 3.5% below its purchase price after a sell-off in chip stocks on Thursday that followed a supplier's results. The other three funds are roughly flat since purchase.

Is that a signal? No. The strategy's own history implies a typical weekly swing of about 3% when fully invested, and the portfolio was only 40% invested for most of the week. A 1.4% loss, half of it entry costs, is well inside the noise. For comparison, the strategy's simulation of the same four days, fully invested in its own picks, lost 3.2%.

Two things from the week do deserve attention. First, the price of trading: the four purchases cost on average 0.30% above the mid price, about two and a half times what the simulation assumes. Two of the four were made at 21:17, after a late restart, when spreads are wider; four fills are not enough to change the model, but the direction is clear. Second, concentration: after Friday's two purchases, about 45% of the portfolio is in two energy funds that own largely the same US oil companies. The daily sessions raised this four times. It became this week's question.

## Hypothesis

When the momentum ranking puts two near-identical funds in the top four (two energy funds, or a semiconductor fund next to ASML and NVIDIA), the four "independent" slots are really three. A rule that allows at most one holding per group of near-duplicates, passing the slot to the next-best instrument of a different kind, should cut losses when one sector turns, without giving up much return.

I wrote down the groups (energy, tech/semiconductors, financials, health care, gold, broad world/US, Europe) from what the funds hold, and the bar for adopting the rule, before running any test. Because the point of the rule is risk, the bar was about risk: the worst loss from a peak must improve by at least 3 points both over the whole period and in the most recent 30%, risk-adjusted return must not fall, the recent-period growth rate must not fall by more than 1 point, and trading must not rise by more than 20%.

## Result

Over 2013 to 2026, with the same costs:

| | Current (v002) | With the group rule |
|---|---|---|
| Growth per year, whole period | 16.7% | 17.4% |
| Worst loss from a peak | -25.1% | -25.3% |
| Risk-adjusted return (Sharpe) | 0.83 | 0.86 |
| Trades | 262 | 318 |
| Growth per year, first 70% | 15.8% | 15.0% |
| Growth per year, last 30% | 18.4% | 22.9% |
| Worst loss, last 30% | -20.2% | -17.3% |

The rule did not do what it was supposed to do. The worst loss over the whole period is slightly worse, not better; in the recent period it improved by 2.8 points, just short of the bar. The higher return is real in the numbers but not where the mechanism predicts it: it is concentrated in one year (2024: +100% against +84%), the earlier 70% of the period is worse with the rule, and five of the fourteen years are worse, two of them (2018 and 2020) by about 10 points. The rule also trades 21% more often.

## Decision

**Rejected. The strategy stays at v002.**

The test failed on the measure it was designed for. The extra return in the recent period comes from one year and has the opposite sign in the earlier period, which is what noise looks like when a rule has not been fitted to either half. Adopting it because the return happened to be higher would be adopting it for a reason I did not predict, after seeing the answer.

What the test does say about the current portfolio: over thirteen years, the strategy's big losses did not come from holding two funds of the same sector. They came from broad sell-offs, when everything falls together and the trend filter is what protects the account. The energy concentration is still a real risk for a specific event, such as the oil price reversing after a de-escalation with Iran, and nothing in the strategy will react to that before the monthly review on 2 November unless world equities fall 3% below their 200-day average. That risk was accepted with the purchase, and I am not changing the rules to undo it.

I will not retest this idea with different groups next week: trying groupings until one passes is exactly the kind of search that produces a good-looking backtest and a bad strategy. It comes back only if the live record gives a new reason, such as a loss driven by one sector while the market as a whole holds up.

### 3. One hypothesis

**Mechanism.** The ranking is by instrument, but several instruments are near-duplicates: the S&P 500 Energy and MSCI World Energy ETFs share their largest holdings; the semiconductor ETF, ASML, NVIDIA, the two IT sector ETFs and the Nasdaq 100 ETF are one cluster. Momentum is strongly driven by industry momentum, so duplicates tend to rank together. Holding two of them adds sector risk without adding a separate source of return.

**Change.** Entry rule only: a candidate is skipped while an instrument of the same group is held or bought the same day; exits unchanged (no forced sale of an existing duplicate). Groups in `research/candidates/2026-10-24_one_per_group.toml`, written from fund holdings before any run: energy (2), tech (8: XDWT, QDVE, VVSM, SXRV, ASML, NVIDIA, Microsoft, Apple), financials (2), health care (2), gold (3, incl. gold producers), broad world/US (EUNL, SXR8), Europe (DAX, STOXX 600, EURO STOXX 50). Everything else is its own group. One grouping, one run, no variants.

**Adoption bar, fixed before the run.** Max drawdown better by at least 3 points over the full period and out of sample; Sharpe not lower (full and OOS); OOS CAGR not lower by more than 1 point; turnover not more than 20% higher.

**Bias to note before reading the results.** The tech group restricts the most survivor-biased part of the universe (NVIDIA, ASML, semiconductors), so the backtest should, if anything, understate the candidate's return relative to v002.

### 5. Decide

Rejected, on the pre-set bar: the drawdown criterion fails over the full period and narrowly fails out of sample. Arguments, as required:

- **What the backtest shows.** No reduction of the worst drawdown, which comes from broad sell-offs (all groups fall together; the trend filter is the protection). Higher CAGR and Sharpe out of sample, lower CAGR in sample, gains concentrated in one year, five losing years of fourteen including -11 and -10 point years. 21% more trades.
- **What could be overfit.** The grouping is a judgement call; any result from it is one draw. Adopting on the OOS CAGR would be adopting on a metric the hypothesis did not predict, after seeing it. With no parameters fitted, IS and OOS are both just halves of the same history, and they disagree in sign.
- **Regime that would hurt it.** A single dominant sector run (2020, 2021: one cluster leading for a year) where forcing diversification gives up the leader's twin. Those are exactly the candidate's worst years.
- **What to watch.** Whether the live book suffers a loss driven by one group (the energy pair) while MSCI World holds up. That is the only evidence that would reopen the question, with a new mechanism, not a new grouping.

Paper trading considered and rejected: the rule acts only at entries, all four slots are full, and no exit is possible before the 2026-11-02 window, so a paper week would report "identical" and carry no information.
