## Hypothesis

The daily sessions asked one question twice this week. The semiconductor fund holds Nvidia as its largest position, and Nvidia had a leadership crisis with its results five days away. The strategy has no way to sell a single holding between its monthly checks unless the whole market breaks down. Would a stop on each holding have helped? And how often would such a stop fire on noise?

This is not the question I declined to reopen last week. That one was whether to act at the monthly check on the month the ranking ignores. This one is whether to act at all between the checks. The mechanism is different: a stop limits how much any one holding can lose before the next check.

The question as asked ("sell at 10% below the purchase price") cannot be tested, because the simulation does not give the strategy its purchase prices. The closest rule that can be tested: **on any day, sell a holding that has closed more than 10% below its highest close of the last month (21 trading days), and do not buy anything in that state.** The second half is needed because the fund just sold is often still ranked first and would otherwise be bought back the next day. Both numbers were fixed before the run: 10% is the daily session's own figure, and one month is the stretch the ranking skips. No other values were tried.

The bar to adopt it, written down before the run, was strict, because a stop trades more and every trade costs money. All five conditions had to hold: the worst fall from a peak at least 3 points better; growth per year over the full period not lower; growth in the most recent 30% of the period not lower; turnover at most 20% higher; and worse than the current strategy in no more than half the calendar years.

## Result

**It failed by a wide margin.** Over 2013–2026, starting from EUR 1,000:

| | Current strategy | With the stop |
|---|---|---|
| Growth per year | 16.1% | 10.9% |
| Worst fall from a peak | -25.1% | -27.4% |
| Growth per year, first 70% | 15.3% | 8.4% |
| Growth per year, last 30% | 17.9% | 17.1% |
| Trades | 264 | 758 |
| Fees paid | EUR 261 | EUR 750 |
| Turnover (times the portfolio, per year) | 3.5 | 12.1 |

With the stop, the simulation grew less and its worst fall was deeper, not shallower. It traded almost three times as often. Fees came to EUR 750, three-quarters of the starting capital. It did better in seven of fourteen years, mostly the quiet ones, and much worse in the trending years that produce most of the strategy's return (2020: 30% instead of 73%; 2021: 18% instead of 42%; 2024: 68% instead of 84%).

On how often it fires on noise: about 500 extra trades in fourteen years, roughly 18 extra sales and re-purchases a year on a four-holding book. Each holding gets stopped out four or five times a year. In this list of instruments, a 10% fall from a recent high is normal and usually temporary. The funds the strategy buys are, by construction, the ones that have been moving the most.

The live case is a good illustration. On 20 October, the day the portfolio bought the semiconductor fund, the fund was already 15% below its high of early October. With the stop, the strategy would never have bought it. That says nothing in the stop's favour or against it: one fund, one month. It does show that a rule like this changes the whole strategy, not just the bad days.

## Decision

**No change. The strategy stays at v002.**

The test answers the question the daily session asked. Over this history, a per-position stop at 10% cost money and did not reduce the worst loss. I chose the rule and its threshold before the run, so I did not pick the best of several. If anything the test favoured the stop: the list of instruments is made of today's winners, which recovered from their falls, and a stop sells exactly those falls. Even so it lost. I will not retest it with a wider stop or a longer window because of a bad day in one fund. That would be choosing the threshold after seeing the result.

What remains true: between 1 December and 1 January the semiconductor fund can fall a long way and the strategy will not act unless the whole market breaks its trend. Nvidia reports on Tuesday 17 November. That is the risk of this design, written down when the fund was bought, and the backtest says that accepting it has paid better than insuring against it. The 1–3 December check is the next point at which the strategy itself can act.

### 3. One hypothesis

Mechanism: 12-1 momentum, with exits only in a 3-day monthly window, leaves a holding unprotected against an idiosyncratic fall for up to a month. A stop caps that per-position loss. The literature on momentum stop-losses (e.g. Han, Zhou and Zhu, 2016, on US single-stock momentum) reports lower crash risk. Against it here: a four-holding book of high-volatility winners, EUR 0.99 per order, and a spread on every round trip.

Change: `research/candidates/2026-11-14_trailing_stop.py` / `.toml`, a copy of v002 plus `[stop] days = 21, max_drawdown = 0.10`. In `signals`, `from_high = close[t] / max(close[t-20..t]) - 1` (only closes up to t). In `generate`, on any day (not only in the window) a held instrument with `from_high < -0.10` is sold, and candidates for purchase must have `from_high >= -0.10`. The trend, ranking, window, sizing and order cap are unchanged.

Why not "from entry": `strategy/backtest.py` builds `Position(...)` without `fifo_price` (line 134), so a stop from entry would see `None` in simulation while working live. That is untestable, and changing the harness is outside a research session. The stop from the 21-day high is the stateless proxy.

Adoption bar (fixed before the run, also written at the top of this file before the backtest): max DD full at least 3 points better (≥ -22.1%); CAGR full ≥ current; CAGR OOS ≥ current; turnover ≤ 1.2 × current; worse in ≤ 7 of 14 years. One parameter set, not swept.
