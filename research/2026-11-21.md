The worst week so far. The portfolio went from EUR 949.49 on Friday 13 November to EUR 889.34 on Friday 20 November, down 6.3%. The strategy can sell single holdings only in the first three trading days of a month, and all four slots were full.

Two separate things went wrong, on different days. On Wednesday oil fell about 3% and the two energy funds lost 2.6% and 2.7% on a day the world market did not move. They are 44% of the portfolio and they move as one. On Thursday and Friday the sell-off in chip and AI shares returned: on Friday the semiconductor fund fell 11.6% in a single day, and it is now 23% below what it cost. Nvidia's results on Tuesday night beat expectations and the fund rose 2% the next day. That did not last.

The simulation of the same rules lost 6.3% over the same five days, the same as the live account to the first decimal. Its holdings are similar but not identical, so the match is partly luck. The point is that this week is not the live account doing something the rules do not do. It is what the rules do in a week like this.

The number to watch is no longer any single fund. On Friday the world market closed only 0.08% above its 200-day average. The rule that sells everything fires after a close more than 3% below that average, about one more Friday-sized fall away.

## Hypothesis

**No hypothesis this week.** The daily sessions asked three questions:

- **Should the strategy avoid holding two near-identical funds, such as the two energy funds?** This was tested on 23–24 October, with groups of similar funds written down before the run. It did not reduce the worst loss and was rejected. Measuring similarity by correlation instead of by group is a different way of drawing the same line, not a new mechanism. Retesting it now, after a bad day for the pair, would be choosing the test because of the result.
- **Should the strategy sell a holding that has fallen a long way between its monthly checks, like the semiconductor fund?** This has been tested twice: on 31 October as a sale at the monthly check after a bad month, and on 14 November as a stop on any day. Both cost money over the fourteen years and neither reduced the worst fall. The semiconductor fund is a painful example of the risk, but it is the risk those two tests already priced. The specific question asked (how often a holding fell more than 20% inside a month, and what holding it cost) cannot be answered from what the simulation keeps: it records the totals of its trades, not each one.
- **Is the daily comparison with the benchmark fair?** No: the portfolio is valued at 18:15 and the benchmark at the 17:30 close, so news after the close lands on one side only. That is a measurement fix, not a strategy change. The daily sessions now report both figures, and the fix in the code is for the owner.

## Decision

**No change. The strategy stays at v002.**

A month into live trading, the portfolio is behind its benchmark, and the two holdings that hurt are the two kinds of risk the strategy openly accepts: concentration in whatever has been rising, and no single-holding exits between monthly checks. Both have been tested against alternatives and the alternatives did worse. Changing the rules now would be reacting to four weeks of results. That is the overfitting this project is supposed to avoid.

What happens next is set by the rules, not by me. If the world market closes below about EUR 118.94, the strategy sells all four holdings over two days and waits in cash. If not, the monthly check on 1–3 December decides whether each holding stays. A fund that has fallen out of the top seven by then will be sold. When that line is crossed, the sale is the design working, not a failure: the band below the average exists so that the strategy does not sell on every small dip.

What would make me reopen the question of concentration: if the live account's losses keep coming from two holdings that move together, week after week, while the simulation's losses do not. So far they have tracked each other.
